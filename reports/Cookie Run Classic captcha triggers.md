# Nobody publicly knows what summons the card check

No public source, official or community, in Korean, Thai or English, says what triggers Cookie Run Classic's "Find the jumping/sliding card" check, and Devsisters has published nothing about it. What the evidence does support is narrower. The check is a descendant of an **anti-macro mini-game that has run in the Korean Kakao build since at least 2014**. Korean players label it 매크로방지 ("anti-macro"), yet it has hit people who were not running any external program. In the global build it appears **between the end of a run and the result screen, not after every run**, in two variants (jumping and sliding), with 2 correct cards out of 6 and 3 tries. The user's view that it is "not really automation detection" is half right. It is plainly an anti-macro measure in intent, but the evidence fits a trigger that works on **play volume, repetitiveness or random sampling**, not one that fires only when it has actually detected a bot. That is why a manual phone player can see it. Korean anecdotes add that emulators and same-pixel tapping seem to raise how often it appears. For the user's MuMu bot, the takeaway is not that the check can be avoided. It will keep appearing, so the bot has to solve both variants reliably and record every occurrence, so the trigger can finally be measured rather than guessed. Every claim below is labelled as verified fact, user report, or inference, and most of the evidence is thin.

## A 2014 Kakao mini-game reborn as the global build's card check

The mechanism is old. A 2014 Hungryapp beginner guide for the Kakao build says that **after macros are used for a certain period, mini-games such as card matching or "pick the jumping cookie" appear and can stop further progress** ([Hungryapp, 2014](https://m.hungryapp.co.kr/bbs/bbs_view.php?bcode=coockirun&pid=577928)). In April 2024 a DC Inside gallery post confirmed that **"a card-draw mini-game already existed for macro prevention,"** and added that some macro users had received **permanent bans (영정)** ([DC 1000564](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1000564)). Korean players have no fixed name for it. They use 미니게임 (mini-game), 매크로방지 (anti-macro), 점프하는 쿠키 찾기 (find the jumping cookie) and 카드뽑기 (card draw). A Fandom post, seen only in a search summary because the page returned an error, describes an occasional "match similar cards" anti-bot puzzle in the Kakao version ([Fandom](https://cookierun.fandom.com/f/p/2502410501095892997)). The idea that the global check is the same family is an **inference** from matching descriptions. I saw no side-by-side screenshots.

The global build's version is better documented, though only through bot code. The upstream max180643 bot's solver assumes **6 cards, of which 2 differ from the other 4**, and it locates the prompt with a single template in a header region ([detection.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/max180643/cookierun-classic-bot/main/detection.py); [config.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/max180643/cookierun-classic-bot/main/config.py)). Upstream issue #4 (31 Aug 2026) reports that the check **"now have 2 (jumping and sliding)"** variants and that it appears **before the result screen**, while the bot only looked for it after ([issue #4](https://github.com/max180643/cookierun-classic-bot/issues/4)). The Thai balagux bot quotes the live prompt "Find the jumping card!" in its notes. Its v1.4.16 solver (30 Sep) reads "jumping" or "sliding" from the header, ranks cards by pose, and tries up to 3 candidates ([balagux releases](https://github.com/balagux/cookierun-classic-bot/releases)). Together these sources show that **Devsisters changed the challenge between July and late August**. That explains the user's 2026-10-11 stall: the user's fork still ships only `ANTI_BOT_1.png`, the original single-variant template, so the bot could not handle the sliding prompt.

## Triggers: two weak signals, many blanks

The table summarises every trigger hypothesis the notes turned up and how strong its support is.

| Hypothesis | Best evidence | Type | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long or high-volume sessions | "After a certain period of use" ([Hungryapp](https://m.hungryapp.co.kr/bbs/bbs_view.php?bcode=coockirun&pid=577928)); appeared after about 20 lives of hands-off play ([DC 1005240](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1005240)) | User reports | Weak to moderate |
| Same-pixel repeated taps | One bot author found the mini-game stopped after randomizing tap positions; the author called the theory "pure guesswork" ([DC 682065](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=682065)) | Single self-test | Weak |
| Emulator / keyboard-mapped input | Very frequent on BlueStacks 5, gone on a tablet ([DC 1027983](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1027983)); appeared on the first run after a fresh BlueStacks install ([DC 1032066](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1032066)) | User reports | Weak |
| Random sampling | Bots with jitter and bots without both meet it; a dev could not force it on demand ([balagux](https://github.com/balagux/cookierun-classic-bot/releases)) | Inference | Consistent, unproven |
| Coins per run (450k) | Tied to a July ban wave, not to the card check ([24buym](https://www.24buym.com/blog/cookie-run-classic)) | Secondhand | No link to the check |
| Time of day, account level, IP, reports | No source | — | None |

The two signals that recur are **duration or volume** and **input that looks mechanical**. Neither has been tested. The same-pixel theory is the most widely believed in bot circles. khm0612's design notes call identical-pixel tapping "a trivial server-side bot tell" ([PROJECT.md](https://github.com/khm0612/cookierun-classic/blob/main/docs/PROJECT.md)), and every open-source bot now adds jitter. Yet the one developer who checked the effect wrote that jitter's **"effectiveness at reducing CAPTCHA prompts is unverified"** ([PacbookMro mobile.6](https://github.com/PacbookMro/cookierun-classic-bot-mobile/releases/tag/v1.1.0-mobile.6)), and jittered bots still meet the check. The 450,000-raw-coins-per-run threshold behind the mid-July 2026 auto-ban wave is a **separate server-side flag**. According to a top-up seller's blog the bans were later reversed, and no source connects that threshold to the card puzzle ([24buym](https://www.24buym.com/blog/cookie-run-classic)).

On frequency, the only number is marketing: a cookierunbot.com sample panel shows the "Card Game" **3 times in 80 runs, all solved** ([cookierunbot.com](https://www.cookierunbot.com/)). That works out to roughly one check per 25–30 runs if it is representative, and nothing shows that it is. No source states a run count, time window or reset rule, and no source says what happens after all 3 tries fail. Bots simply stop.

## The manual-play sighting fits, but it is not proof of innocence

The user's doubt deserves a fair hearing, and the evidence partly supports it. The check is **not proof that automation was detected**. Korean players met it during 손크로 ("hand-macro"), where they pressed start by hand with no external tool and let the cookie's combo run itself ([DC 1000564](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1000564); [DC 1005240](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1005240)). Fresh emulator installs saw it on the very first run, before any long pattern could build up. Bot vendors treat it as inevitable: cookieafk says it solves the check "so runs never stall" ([cookieafk.com](https://cookieafk.com/en)). That is how a designer would build a **cheap probabilistic or heuristic gate**: shown now and then to everyone, more often to suspicious-looking sessions, and expensive only for scripts that cannot solve it.

The steelman for the opposite view is also real. Devsisters plainly built the check for anti-macro purposes, Korean players name it that way, and permanent bans of Kakao macro users are reported ([DC 1000564](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1000564)). A risk-scored trigger can fire for a human, especially one who plays many runs in a row, and still fire much more often for a bot. Two gaps keep this unresolved. I found **no Korean, Thai or English report from an actively playing manual player**, so the user's own sighting is the only manual data point. And the place where such reports would live was unreachable: DC Inside comment threads did not load, namu.wiki, thewiki, theqoo and ppomppu returned 403, and Reddit, Discord, Facebook groups and TikTok are poorly indexed. My overall verdict is that the check is anti-automation in intent and is probably not a pure detection verdict in practice. Seeing it once on a phone does not mean the bot's sightings are harmless, and it does not mean they are evidence of a flag.

## What this means for the MuMu bot: measure, solve, stay boring

The user's bot already has most of the features the community associates with lower check rates: ±15 px tap jitter, a randomized 0.3–0.55 s jump rhythm, 10–25 s waits between games and app restarts every 1.5–3 h. None of these has been shown to change how often the check appears. Two Korean signals deserve honest acknowledgement. MuMu is an emulator, and emulators appeared in the highest-frequency reports. And the bot plays many consecutive runs, which matches the "period of use" trigger. Its between-game wait is also shorter than upstream's 30–60 s ([max180643 README](https://github.com/max180643/cookierun-classic-bot)). Whether any of that matters is **unknown**, and that gap is exactly what the user's own data can fill.

The highest-value step is **logging every check occurrence**. `coin_ledger.py` writes records.csv through a fixed `RECORD_FIELDS` list with `csv.DictWriter`, so a new column needs a one-time header migration. Otherwise, adding a header to an existing file would misalign old rows. The simplest safe options are to append a row with `status="anti_bot"`, which needs no schema change, or to add explicit fields while rewriting the header once. Each occurrence should record the timestamp, the variant (jumping or sliding, read from the header), the result (pass on try 1, 2 or 3, fail, or stuck), the runs and minutes since bot or session start, the runs and minutes since the last app restart, the runs and minutes since the previous check, the coins from the run that preceded it, and the current version tag. After a few hundred runs this answers the questions no forum could. Is the check per-run random, does it cluster late in sessions, does a restart reset it, and do changes to the wait or jitter move it? Because records.csv already tags each run with a version, comparing check rates across settings becomes a simple query. A manual tally of phone sightings would give a useful human baseline alongside it.

The second step is **keeping the solver current for both variants**. The 40-minute stall on 2026-10-11 came from a single-template detector meeting the sliding prompt. The fix is to add templates for both headers (or OCR the word "jumping" or "sliding"), detect the check before the result screen as issue #4 advises, pick the minority animation group as the answer pair (khm0612 and balagux both describe this rule), and stop safely after at most 3 attempts. That last point matters because khm0612 warns that **"wrong card taps cost more than pausing"** ([PROJECT.md](https://github.com/khm0612/cookierun-classic/blob/main/docs/PROJECT.md)). The bot should also save a screenshot and raise an alert, such as a desktop notification or sound, whenever it is unsure, so a human can step in within minutes rather than 40. Watching the balagux and upstream release pages catches future variant changes early.

Ban risk should be stated plainly. Devsisters ran a **Classic "Fair Play Campaign" around July 2026** warning that listed activities "may result in a permanent account suspension". The list itself was in an image I could not fetch ([X/CRC_news](https://x.com/CRC_news/status/2074502714608759225)). The Kakao precedent includes permanent bans for macro use ([DC 1000564](https://gall.dcinside.com/mgallery/board/view/?id=cookierun&no=1000564)). I found **no public report of a Classic ban for botting specifically**, and no evidence that passing or failing the card check feeds into bans. Any ADB bot remains a terms-of-service violation whatever the check does. This report recommends no evasion services, device spoofing or cheats. The goal is a bot that handles a known screen correctly and data that lets the user judge the risk for themselves.

## Conclusion

The search changed the question. It no longer makes sense to ask what the secret trigger is. The useful questions are whether the check is a sampled speed bump or a targeted flag, and whether the user's own play pattern moves its rate. No one has published a measurement, not even the bot vendors who solve it thousands of times. A logged records.csv would make the user's bot the first source anywhere with real trigger data. The practical risk on 2026-10-11 was never detection. It was a stale template that left the bot frozen on a screen it should have handled in seconds.

The thin record also means confident claims online should be read sceptically, including ones like "jitter prevents it", "restarts reset it" and "it means you're flagged". They rest on one 2022 self-test, a handful of 2024 Kakao anecdotes and vendor marketing. Data the user collects themselves will soon outweigh all of them.
